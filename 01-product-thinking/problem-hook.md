- **Problem hook, summarize the business risk and user pain into one urgent sentence.:** Nearly one in five new instalment plans collapses within 60 days, hitting shift workers and people with several open claims hardest, because people choose plans they can’t afford without help, and each broken plan costs the consumer another round of letters and the client another complaint.
- **Value proposition, based on the initiative in your assigned Riverty case, what is the new value and why is it urgent to launch now?:** I only have the data pack, not the brief itself, so I’m taking “the initiative” to be the affordability check we’ve been working on. If the brief frames it differently, tell me and I’ll adjust.

Value proposition

For consumers with tight or irregular income and open claims, the affordability check helps you pick an instalment plan you can keep, in a few calm steps and with clear consequences, so you avoid missed payments and more letters. Unlike today’s plan-first portal, it starts from your situation and explains why it asks.

The new value, by stakeholder

Consumer: A plan that fits their income pattern and pay day, plus a plain explanation of why income is asked and what happens after a missed payment. This answers the top agent-contact reasons (482 and 419 contacts in the sample month).
Client: Fewer broken plans and fewer complaints, which N-10 says clients watch as closely as recovery.
Agent: Less time on the same questions, and a view of what the consumer entered and why a plan was offered (N-08, N-09).

Why launch now

The trend is moving the wrong way. Missed payments rose from 15.6% in Month 3 to 19.0% in Month 6 after the 6-month-first change, and are still creeping up. That is about 130 extra broken plans a month versus the Month 3 rate (my calculation), and the cost is paid by the people least able to absorb it.
The headline hides the stall. Agreements look healthy at 46.0%, but kept agreements have been flat at about 3,100 a month since Month 2. Without a decision, this stays invisible until a client raises it.
Each month of delay repeats the problem. About 3,850 new agreements a month means around 730 more plans likely to fail (3,846 × 19%) for every month the portal stays as it is.
The people affected are concentrated. Variable-income users miss 22.5% of payments versus 17.4% for fixed salaries, so a first pilot can target one measurable segment.

The urgency argument rests on the pack’s trends. The pack has nothing on regulatory deadlines, competitors or contract renewal dates, so I can’t claim an external trigger. If the brief or the client has one, it would strengthen the case.

The agreement rate may fall slightly with an added step, so success should be judged on kept plans, not on agreements alone.

The agreement rate is still improving, so on the surface the business is performing. Most people in the funnel aren’t hurt by the problem.
The effect is incremental, around 19% of 3,846 agreements, so the loss builds up gradually.
Euro values are missing. Without recovery per plan and the cost of an agent contact, I can’t turn broken plans into money.

A pilot-sized initiative: one segment (variable income is the strongest candidate), a defined test against the 19% baseline, and a guardrail on agreement rate and complaints. The case is for learning and fixing a known problem, not for a large programme.

It becomes high-stakes if missed payments pass roughly 20-22% for several months, if complaints start rising, if a client raises it at renewal, or if a regulator or compliance colleague flags the default plan order.

Why it could turn out generic

Any questionnaire can be called an affordability check. The value comes from the details: ranges instead of exact figures, “typical month” vs. “this month”, pay-day dates, a skip option, and consequences shown on the plan. Without them, it is an extra step with the same unclear income question.
It might not beat a cheaper fix. After the 6-month plan became the first option in Month 4, missed payments jumped from 15.6% to 18.4%. Reversing the default is much cheaper and may recover much of that. That is my inference from the trend, not tested. The check should therefore be tested against a reversed default, not only against today’s baseline.
It rests on an unproven assumption: that consumers’ answers are accurate enough to choose a plan well. The pack doesn’t say, and variable income is exactly where self-reporting is hardest.

What makes it specific

It targets the segment with the worst numbers (variable income, 22.5% missed payments).
It changes the order of the journey: situation first, plan second. That is a real change from today.
It ties the check to the top contact reasons, so you can measure whether those contacts fall.

A sharper version of the value proposition
“Show only plans that fit your income pattern and pay day, and explain what happens if you miss one, so shift workers stop agreeing to plans they can’t keep.”

To be fair to the case, I’d run a three-way test: today’s journey, a reversed plan order alone, and the full check. If reversing the order recovers most of the gain, the check’s value is smaller than I claimed. If the check clearly beats it, especially for variable income, the case is strong. I can write that up as a test plan if you’d like.
